## Chapter 1: Introduction

The continuous development of resin-based composite materials has expanded the range of clinical applications for both direct and indirect restorations. Among the properties that most strongly influence long-term clinical success, wear resistance and surface roughness occupy a central position. Excessive wear can result in loss of occlusal morphology, reduction of vertical dimension, and deterioration of aesthetics. Increased surface roughness, on the other hand, promotes plaque accumulation, staining, and secondary caries.

In recent years a new generation of highly filled injectable composites has been introduced. These materials combine the handling advantages of traditional flowable resins with filler loadings that approach those of conventional paste-type nanohybrid composites. Manufacturers claim that they are suitable for load-bearing restorations. Independent comparative data on their behaviour under controlled three-body abrasion that simulates daily toothbrushing remain relatively scarce.

An arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) value above 0.2 µm is generally associated with increased biofilm formation, while values above 0.5 µm can be detected by the patient’s tongue. Wear and surface roughness are closely linked: progressive loss of the resin-rich surface layer during toothbrushing exposes the underlying filler architecture and can markedly alter surface topography.

Although many studies have examined the wear and surface characteristics of conventional nanohybrid composites, fewer investigations have directly compared them with the newer highly filled injectable materials under identical laboratory conditions. A controlled evaluation is therefore needed.

### 1.1 Aim of the Study

**Primary aim**  
To compare the wear resistance and surface roughness of highly filled injectable composite resins with those of a conventional nanohybrid composite resin after simulated toothbrushing.

**Secondary aim**  
To determine the percentage change in weight and surface roughness (Ra) of the tested materials following a standardised three-body abrasion protocol approximating one year of clinical toothbrushing.

## Chapter 2: Review of Literature

### 2.1 Wear Resistance

Wear is defined by the Institution of Mechanical Engineers as the progressive loss of substance resulting from mechanical interaction between two contacting surfaces that are in relative motion (1). In the mouth, dental wear includes the cumulative loss of both natural tooth tissue and restorative materials from processes unrelated to caries or fracture (2).

Wear resistance is considered an essential physical property because it helps predict the clinical longevity of restorative materials. Excessive wear may lead to loss of occlusal vertical dimension, premature anterior contacts, reduced chewing efficiency, muscle fatigue, and compromised appearance (3). For this reason wear resistance is frequently used as a screening criterion when selecting resin composites for the replacement of tooth structure.

Clinically, wear is usually classified according to its manifestation: attrition (wear at sites of direct occlusal contact), abrasion (wear at non-contact sites), and corrosion (chemically mediated wear) (4). Abrasive wear itself is divided into two-body abrasion, caused by hard asperities on the opposing surface, and three-body abrasion, mediated by loose abrasive particles between the surfaces (5). Occlusal contact points mainly experience two-body abrasion, while non-contact areas are subjected to three-body abrasion by food particles or toothpaste abrasives (6).

The wear behaviour of resin composites is complex. It is influenced primarily by filler characteristics (size, shape, distribution and volume fraction), the composition and degree of conversion of the resin matrix, and the quality of the filler–matrix interface (7,8). Studies have shown that abrasive wear decreases when filler particle size or inter-particle spacing is reduced, when the degree of conversion is increased, and when silane coupling is improved (9). In flowable materials, filler size often exerts a greater influence on wear than the mechanical properties of the resin matrix alone; the incorporation of dense nanoparticles or spherical submicron fillers can improve performance (10).

Fatigue wear arises from repeated cyclic loading that generates subsurface microcracks and eventual particle detachment (11). Sliding contact produces alternating zones of compression and tension that contribute to this process (12).

Laboratory evaluation of wear follows standardised two-body and three-body protocols described in ISO technical specifications (13). Toothbrushing simulation is particularly useful for assessing three-body abrasive wear under conditions that approximate daily oral hygiene. Wear can be quantified by profilometry, three-dimensional scanning, or simple gravimetric weight loss:

Weight loss = W1 − W2

where W1 and W2 are the specimen masses before and after testing (14).

Although laboratory tests allow rapid ranking of materials, they cannot fully reproduce the multifactorial conditions of the oral cavity. Clinical assessment has traditionally relied on the USPHS (Ryge) criteria and their later modifications, as well as indirect methods using sequential impressions and casts (15).

### 2.2 Surface Roughness

Surface topography is a fundamental determinant of both the aesthetic performance and biological compatibility of resin-based composite restorations (16). An unsatisfactory surface smoothness can lead to plaque accumulation, secondary caries at the margins, increased crevicular fluid flow, extrinsic staining, and loss of gloss (17,18).

The arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) is the parameter most frequently reported. A value of approximately 0.2 µm is widely accepted as the threshold above which bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation increase significantly (19,20). Surfaces smoother than this threshold show no further clinically relevant reduction in plaque. Roughness values exceeding 0.5 µm are generally detectable by the tip of the tongue and may cause patient dissatisfaction (19). More recent work continues to support the 0.2 µm threshold for minimising plaque retention on restorative materials (21).

Rough surfaces also facilitate extrinsic staining. Streptococcus mutans can further roughen composite surfaces through metabolic activity, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of biofilm accumulation and surface degradation (22).

Several factors contribute to progressive surface roughening in the oral environment. Immersion in acidic or alcoholic beverages causes water sorption and chemical softening of the resin matrix, exposing filler particles and often doubling initial Ra values (23). Simulated toothbrush abrasion is another major contributor. Loss of the resin-rich surface layer reveals the underlying filler architecture; the strength of the filler–matrix bond then determines the extent of particle plucking (24). Toothpastes with higher relative dentin abrasivity (RDA > 100) and larger abrasive particles accelerate gloss loss and roughness increase (1). Surface degradation increases roughly linearly with the number of brushing cycles, yet the final texture is governed mainly by the material’s monomer and filler system rather than by toothbrush stiffness alone (25).

Material composition has a decisive influence. Nanofilled composites tend to show more uniform wear of agglomerated nanoparticles, resulting in milder increases in roughness and better gloss retention after toothbrushing than nanohybrid materials, in which larger fillers are more readily exposed or dislodged (26).

Surface roughness is measured by both contact (stylus) profilometry and non-contact optical methods such as confocal laser scanning microscopy. Contact profilometry remains a reliable technique for determining Ra and Rz, with vertical resolution approaching 0.5 nm (27,28). Optical methods offer the advantage of non-contact measurement and the generation of three-dimensional topographic maps.

### 2.3 Injectable Composite Resins

Highly filled injectable composite resins represent a relatively recent development intended to overcome the traditional limitations of conventional low-viscosity flowable materials. Traditional flowables usually contain lower filler loadings (approximately 40–55 wt%) and consequently show inferior mechanical properties, higher polymerisation shrinkage and reduced wear resistance (29,30).

Modern injectable composites incorporate substantially higher inorganic filler contents, often ranging from 50 % to nearly 70 % by weight. This is achieved through the use of nano-sized or submicron spherical fillers together with optimised rheological modifiers. The result is a material that retains an injectable consistency while approaching the mechanical performance of conventional paste-type composites (31,32). Representative commercial examples include G-ænial Universal Injectable (GC) and Beautifil Flow Plus X / Beautifil Injectable (Shofu). Both are marketed for use in load-bearing as well as non-load-bearing restorations.

Recent laboratory studies have reported favourable mechanical properties for several of these materials. Under two-body wear testing, certain highly filled injectable formulations have shown higher flexural strength and lower volumetric wear than some conventional nanohybrid paste composites (33,34). Surface roughness values after simulated toothbrushing or chewing simulation have also been reported as comparable to, and in some cases lower than, those of conventional nanohybrid materials (35,36). Scanning electron microscopy of worn surfaces frequently reveals more uniform filler distribution and less particle plucking in the nano-filled injectable resins.

Despite these promising laboratory findings, the long-term behaviour of highly filled injectable composites under three-body abrasion conditions that closely simulate daily toothbrushing is still less extensively documented than that of conventional nanohybrid materials. Differences in filler morphology, silane treatment and resin matrix composition among commercial products can produce material-dependent results, which underlines the need for direct comparative evaluations under standardised protocols.

### 2.4 Conventional Nanohybrid Composite Resins

Conventional nanohybrid composite resins remain the clinical reference standard for direct restorations in both anterior and posterior regions. These materials typically contain a mixture of nano-sized particles (approximately 5–100 nm) and larger micron- or submicron-sized fillers, resulting in total inorganic filler loadings that commonly exceed 70–80 wt% (37,38). The dual particle-size distribution is intended to optimise packing density, improve polishability and enhance mechanical properties such as flexural strength, fracture toughness and wear resistance.

The higher filler volume fraction and more rigid filler network of nanohybrid composites generally confer superior resistance to abrasive wear compared with traditional low-filled flowable resins (39). Under both two-body and three-body wear conditions, conventional nanohybrid materials have consistently shown lower volumetric loss and more stable surface topography than conventional flowables (40). After proper finishing and polishing, surface roughness usually remains within or near the clinically acceptable threshold of 0.2 µm (41).

The higher viscosity of paste-type nanohybrid composites can, however, create handling difficulties, especially in deep or narrow cavities, and may increase the risk of void incorporation. The development of highly filled injectable composites was partly motivated by the desire to combine the favourable mechanical properties of nanohybrid materials with the superior adaptation and ease of placement of flowable resins.

Comparative studies that evaluate highly filled injectable and conventional nanohybrid composites under identical wear and surface-roughness protocols are still relatively limited. Existing data suggest that certain injectable formulations can approach, and under specific test conditions even match, the performance of nanohybrid paste composites, yet material-specific differences persist (33,35,36). A controlled laboratory comparison of wear resistance and surface roughness changes after standardised three-body toothbrushing simulation is therefore required to clarify the relative performance of these two material categories under clinically relevant abrasive conditions.
